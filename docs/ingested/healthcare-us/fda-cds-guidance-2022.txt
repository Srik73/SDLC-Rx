NOTE: This guidance interprets section 520(o)(1)(E) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), as added by Section 3060(a) of the 21st Century Cures Act (2016). It governs whether a CDS software function is regulated by FDA as a medical device ("Device CDS") or excluded from that definition ("Non-Device CDS"). This determination is directly relevant to any CDS alerting feature: it decides whether the feature triggers FDA medical device regulatory obligations (premarket review, quality system regulation, adverse event reporting) in addition to ONC certification and general software engineering practice.

THE FOUR STATUTORY CRITERIA (section 520(o)(1)(E) of the FD&C Act):
A software function must meet ALL FOUR of the following criteria to be considered Non-Device CDS (i.e., excluded from FDA medical device regulation). Failing any one criterion means the function is Device CDS and subject to FDA oversight.

Criterion 1 — Not intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image, a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device, or a pattern/signal from a signal acquisition system.
FDA's guidance clarifies that software functions which assess or interpret the clinical implications or clinical relevance of a signal, pattern, or medical image do NOT meet Criterion 1 (because they "acquire, process, or analyze" such data) and are therefore subject to FDA regulation.

Criterion 2 — Intended to display, analyze, or print medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies, clinical practice guidelines, drug or device labeling, or government agency recommendations), rather than acquiring/processing/analyzing such information itself.

Criterion 3 — Intended to support or provide recommendations to a health care professional about the prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition.

Criterion 4 — Intended to enable the health care professional to independently review the basis for the recommendations presented, so that the professional does not rely primarily on the software's recommendation to make a clinical decision for an individual patient. The final guidance narrowed this criterion significantly compared to the 2019 draft — FDA raised specific concern about "automation bias," where a provider defaults to assuming a CDS output is correct without independently reviewing its basis.

PRACTICAL IMPLICATION FOR SOFTWARE REQUIREMENTS: A CDS alerting function that (a) reasons over problem lists, medication lists, allergy data, lab results, or vital signs (rather than raw signals/images), (b) surfaces recommendations rather than acting autonomously, and (c) exposes its underlying evidence/basis to the clinician in a way that supports independent judgment, has a stronger case for Non-Device CDS status. A function that outputs a diagnosis, risk score, or treatment recommendation without exposing a reviewable basis — or that a reasonable clinician would be expected to simply accept — is more likely Device CDS and subject to FDA's medical device framework (which would materially change SDLC requirements: design controls under 21 CFR Part 820, premarket submission pathways, and formal risk management under ISO 14971 become relevant considerations).

The final 2022 guidance is explicitly more expansive in FDA's assertion of regulatory scope than the 2019 draft guidance it replaced — multiple products previously treated as Non-Device CDS under the draft guidance are expected to be classified as Device CDS (and thus FDA-regulated) under the final guidance. This shift itself is a documented source of industry pushback (see e.g. April 2024 congressional letter questioning FDA's evidentiary basis for the expanded scope), which is itself a useful NEEDS-VALIDATION flag for any CDS requirement: FDA's device/non-device line for a specific alerting feature should be confirmed against the current guidance and, where ambiguous, treated as a regulatory risk requiring legal/regulatory affairs review rather than assumed.
